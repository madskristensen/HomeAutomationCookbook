How to use this guide

A few minutes here makes the rest of the month easier.

Chapter 2

The rules for a starter house

Five simple rules. They are the difference between a house that feels random and one your family relies on.

  1. Pick one hub and stick with it.Three apps on day one means three places to look when something breaks.
  2. Choose local first.Pick a hub and devices that run locally, so they keep working when the internet goes down. Z-Wave, Zigbee, Matter over Thread, and HomeKit are all good examples.
  3. Keep the wall switches working.Every light keeps a normal wall control. Guests, grandparents and babysitters should never need an app.
  4. One room at a time.Finish a room, live with it for a week, then move on. A small win you trust beats a big project you're always fixing.
  5. Save the risky stuff for later.Leave locks, heaters, and garage door control for later. You will be ready for them once the basics feel boring.
Keep this handy

If you ever feel stuck, come back to this page. Almost every problem in a first month traces back to breaking one of these five rules.

Chapter 3

Pick the hub

The hub is the brain of the house. The good news: you do not need the perfect one. You need one you will keep.

Home Assistant, Hubitat, SmartThings, Homey, HomeSeer, Apple Home, Google Home, and Alexa can all run this plan. The best pick runs your rules locally and supports the devices you want. If you already own one, start there.

Check the radios

Devices talk to the hub over a radio type. Z-Wave devices need a hub with a Z-Wave radio. Zigbee devices need a hub with a Zigbee radio. Apple Home and Google Home have neither, and Alexa has Zigbee only on some Echo models. On those platforms, look for Matter versions of each device. The shopping list marks the radio type for every item.

Do these three things on day one

  • Give the hub a fixed IP address on your router.
  • Turn on its backups.
  • Install the hub's app on at least two phones, so alerts reach more than one person.

Read more: www.homeautomationcookbook.com/getting-started/choose-the-right-platform.html

Naming devices

Name every device by room and job. "Laundry Washer Power" is better than "Zooz ZEN15 plug". Good names make every rule easier to read, and every alert easier to act on.

Mixing radio types is fine if your hub supports them. Mains-powered switches and plugs usually act as repeaters and make the mesh stronger. Install those before the battery sensors in far rooms.

What you will have working

A hub that is set up, backed up and reachable from two phones. That is a real foundation.

LightingWalkthrough 1 of 11

Lights on motion

The light comes on before your hand reaches the switch. The switch still works.

Why this matters

This is the recipe that makes a house feel smart. It is also the easiest one to get right, so it is a great first win.

Steps

  1. Install the switch first. Check that the paddle works like a normal switch.
  2. Pair the motion sensor. Name it by room and job, for example "Hallway Motion".
  3. Mount the sensor where it sees the path into the room, not the wall switch.
  4. Create the rule. Trigger: motion becomes active.
  5. Add a condition: the light is off. This keeps the rule from changing a level someone picked.
  6. Add a second condition: the room is dark, or it is inside your night hours.
  7. Action: turn on the light. At night use 10 to 20 percent. In the evening use a useful level, such as 60 percent.
  8. Do not add a turn-off step yet. That is the next walkthrough.
Test it

Walk in from the dark at night. The light should be on before you reach the switch. Then walk in on a bright day. It should stay off.

Tip

Use your hub's simplest rule tool for a single on action. Many hubs also have a motion lighting app. Room Lighting on Hubitat is one example.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

LightingWalkthrough 2 of 11

Lights off after motion

The light turns off after the room has been empty for a while, but never while someone is still there.

Why this matters

A light that goes dark on someone reading is the fastest way to lose the family's trust. A patient timeout keeps everyone happy.

Steps

  1. Live with walkthrough 1 for a week before you add this one.
  2. Trigger: the room sensor has reported no motion for the timeout.
  3. Start the timeout at 5 to 10 minutes for a bathroom or quiet room. Use 15 minutes if people sit still.
  4. Condition: no other sensor in the same space is active. If a closet opens into the room, watch both sensors.
  5. Condition: no manual override. If someone used the wall switch, leave the light alone.
  6. Optional condition: music is not playing in the room. Playing music is a good sign someone is still there.
  7. Action: turn the light off.
  8. Make sure new motion cancels the countdown.
Test it

Sit still in the room for longer than the timeout. If the light goes off on you, raise the timeout before you blame the sensor.

Tip

Shorten the timeout only after the household has lived with it for a while.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

LightingWalkthrough 3 of 11

Bathroom night-light

A dim, warm light at night so nobody is blinded at 2 AM.

Why this matters

Small comforts like this are what make people love the system. Everyone in the house will notice it on the first night.

Steps

  1. Install the dimmer and pair the multisensor.
  2. Trigger: bathroom or entry motion is detected.
  3. Condition: the mode is Night, or the time is inside your night window.
  4. Optional condition: the room is dark enough, using the sensor's light reading.
  5. Action: set the bathroom light to 10 percent. Add a warm color only if the light supports it.
  6. Let walkthrough 2 own the turn-off. Start at 10 minutes for a shower bathroom.
Test it

Walk in at night with the house in Night mode. The light should come on dim. A press on the switch should still bring full light.

Tip

If you later add the shower fan recipe, the same multisensor gives you the humidity reading.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

AppliancesWalkthrough 4 of 11

Washer done alert

One alert when the washer is really done. No alerts during pauses in the cycle.

Why this matters

No more damp clothes forgotten in the drum. A few minutes of measuring now gives you an alert you can trust for years.

Check your hub

The Zooz ZEN15 is Z-Wave. It needs a hub with a Z-Wave radio and will not work with Apple Home, Google Home or Alexa. Alternative for Apple Home, Google Home or Alexa: any Matter or Zigbee smart plug with energy monitoring that your hub supports. Check that it is rated for your washer's motor load. Many small plugs are not.

Steps

  1. Plug the washer into the washer power-monitoring plug. Check that the washer is within the plug's rated load.
  2. Run three loads with logging only. Write down the power while running, during pauses, and when finished.
  3. Pick a running threshold that is clearly above idle power. Pick a finished threshold just above the idle level.
  4. Rule A. Trigger: power stays above the running threshold long enough to prove a cycle started. Action: set a "Washer running" marker.
  5. Rule B. Trigger: power stays below the finished threshold longer than the longest normal pause.
  6. Rule B condition: the "Washer running" marker is set.
  7. Rule B actions: send one "Washer finished" notification, set "Laundry waiting", and clear the running marker.
  8. Never let the rule turn the plug off.
Test it

Run a normal load and watch your phone. You should get exactly one alert, a few minutes after the washer stops.

Tip

Some hubs have an app that tracks running and finished states for you. Appliance Cycle Monitor on Hubitat is one example.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

AppliancesWalkthrough 5 of 11

Dryer done alert

One alert when the dryer stops shaking for good.

Why this matters

Dryers pause and tumble during cooldown. Measuring one load first is what keeps this to a single, useful alert.

Steps

  1. Stick the vibration sensor on top of the dryer, away from the hot vent.
  2. Run a load and note how long vibration stops during normal pauses or cooldown.
  3. Rule A. Trigger: vibration stays active for the start delay. Action: set a "Dryer running" marker.
  4. Rule B. Trigger: vibration stays inactive longer than the longest pause you saw.
  5. Rule B conditions: the "Dryer running" marker is set, and the sensor is online.
  6. Rule B actions: send one "Dryer finished" notification, set "Laundry waiting", and clear the marker.
Test it

Run a full load, including any wrinkle guard. You should get one alert, not three.

Tip

If you get alerts during cooldown, the finish delay is too short.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

SafetyWalkthrough 6 of 11

Water leak alert

An alert at any hour that says exactly where the water is.

Why this matters

A small leak caught early is a towel. A leak found days later is a repair bill. This one is worth doing carefully.

Steps

  1. Name each sensor by place, for example "Leak Water Heater" and "Leak Washer".
  2. Place them flat on the floor where water shows up first.
  3. Trigger: any leak sensor reports wet.
  4. No conditions. Leak alerts run in Home, Away, Night, and Guest modes.
  5. Action: send a high-priority notification that names the sensor. Send it to at least two people.
  6. Optional: repeat the alert only while nobody has checked on it.
  7. Clear the incident by hand, after a person has looked at the source. A dry sensor does not mean the leak is fixed.
Test it

Touch the sensor with a wet towel. Check that both phones get the alert and that the message names the right place.

Tip

Leave automatic water shutoff for later. Get trusted alerts first.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

LightingWalkthrough 7 of 11

Closet light on door open

The closet or pantry light comes on when the door opens.

Why this matters

It is a simple recipe that gets used many times a day. It is also a gentle way to learn how contact sensors behave.

Steps

  1. Mount the contact sensor on the door and frame with the two parts lined up.
  2. Trigger: the contact changes from closed to open.
  3. Condition: the space is dark enough, or it is inside the hours when light makes sense.
  4. Action: turn on the light at the level you want.
  5. Optional second rule: turn the light off when the door closes, or after a timeout.
  6. Do not undo a manual switch change right away.
Test it

Open the door ten times in a row. The light should come on every time.

Tip

If it misses, check the gap between the two sensor parts.

SafetyWalkthrough 8 of 11

Garage door left open

A reminder when the garage door has been open longer than expected.

Why this matters

An open garage door at night is easy to miss and easy to fix. A friendly reminder covers it without any risky automation.

Steps

  1. Mount the tilt sensor on the top panel of the garage door.
  2. Trigger: the door reports open and stays open for 10 minutes.
  3. Action: send "Garage door has been open for 10 minutes".
  4. Second rule. Trigger: 10:00 PM. Condition: the door is open. Action: send a high-priority "Garage door still open at bedtime".
  5. Do not close the door from this recipe. Closing stays manual.
Test it

Open the garage door and wait 10 minutes. Then close it and make sure no more alerts arrive.

Tip

Auto-closing is a separate, later project. It needs an opener that supports safe unattended operation.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

NotificationsWalkthrough 9 of 11

Low-battery alerts

No sensor ever goes quiet without anyone knowing.

Why this matters

A dead battery is the most common reason a good rule stops working. This alert quietly protects every other recipe.

Steps

  1. Add every battery device to one list.
  2. Trigger: a battery drops below its threshold, or reports low battery.
  3. Condition: this device has not been flagged in the last 7 days.
  4. Action: send "[Device name] battery is at [level]".
  5. The alert resets when the battery is replaced and reports normal again.
Test it

Put a weak battery in one sensor. You should get one alert, not one every hour.

Tip

Keep spare batteries of each size in one drawer.

Daily routinesWalkthrough 10 of 11

Away mode

The house switches to Away only when it is really empty.

Why this matters

Away mode ties the whole house together. Being careful here means nobody at home ever gets left in the dark.

Steps

  1. Set up presence for each person and test it for a week.
  2. Trigger: the last tracked person leaves.
  3. Condition: everyone has been away for 15 minutes.
  4. Condition: Guest mode is off, and no recent indoor motion suggests someone is home.
  5. Actions: set the mode to Away, turn off lights that are safe to turn off, and send a summary.
  6. Cancel it if someone returns, or if a person picks Home or Guest.
Test it

Leave together and check that Away is set after the delay. Then leave one person home and check that it is not.

Tip

Shorten the 15 minutes only after several weeks without a false Away.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

Daily routinesWalkthrough 11 of 11

Bedtime and Night mode

Night mode from one intentional action, with an automatic backup.

Why this matters

Going to bed should be one tap. The backup rule catches the nights everyone forgets, without ever surprising someone still up.

Steps

  1. Manual rule. Trigger: a double tap down on a stair or hallway switch.
  2. Conditions: the mode is Home, and the time is between 8 PM and midnight.
  3. Action: set the mode to Night and turn off approved unoccupied lights. Keep path lighting.
  4. Automatic backup. At 10 PM, if the mode is Home, check every 10 minutes, up to 12 times.
  5. Each pass: stop if the mode is no longer Home. If every downstairs motion sensor has been inactive for 15 minutes, set Night and stop.
  6. If people are still up after about two hours, stay in Home until someone sets Night by hand.
  7. Report anything that still needs a person, like an open garage door.
Test it

Double tap the switch and check that the mode changes. On another night, go to bed without it and check that the backup sets Night.

Tip

The double tap is the main action. The automatic rule only catches the nights when everyone forgets.

You have got this. If the test fails, change one setting at a time and try again.

Chapter 8

Add modes, then routines

Modes are where the house starts to feel calm and joined up.

Once the devices work on their own, add home modes. Day, Evening, Night, and Away are enough.

Modes make every later recipe simpler. A rule can check one mode instead of five conditions.

Build Away mode and the bedtime routine (walkthroughs 10 and 11) only after the room recipes have run well for a few weeks.

What you will have working

Four modes that every rule can lean on, and a house that knows when you are home, away or asleep.

Chapter 10

Troubleshooting

Something not quite right? That is normal. Most fixes take a few minutes.

ProblemFirst thing to try
Light turns on after you reach the switchMove the sensor so it sees the entry path earlier.
Light turns on in daylightAdd a light-level condition. Test on a bright overcast day too.
Light turns off while someone sits stillRaise the timeout, or watch a second sensor.
Washer or dryer alert comes more than onceThe finish delay is shorter than a normal pause. Make it longer.
No washer alert at allCheck that the "running" marker is set during a load.
A battery sensor drops offAdd a mains-powered switch or plug between it and the hub.
A device will not pair at allCheck that your hub has the radio the device needs (Z-Wave, Zigbee or Matter).
A rule stops working when the internet is downCheck that the devices and rule run locally on the hub.

Change one thing at a time, then test again. It is slower for a minute and much faster overall.
